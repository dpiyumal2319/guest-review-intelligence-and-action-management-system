\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}
A five-star hotel's reputation is no longer written only by its front desk. Increasingly it is
mediated by algorithms, such as search rankings and conversational AI assistants, that read guest
reviews and distil them into a one-line verdict. When a recurring complaint is repeated by enough
guests it becomes a permanent caveat in how the property is described on every query. The Kingsbury,
Colombo, a five-star hotel rated 4.0/5 and ranked \#32 of 103 hotels in Colombo on TripAdvisor,
illustrates the problem. Thousands of reviews are spread across multiple platforms, far beyond what
any operations team can read, cross-reference and act on consistently.
This report presents a working proof-of-concept that runs the same mechanism that judges the hotel,
but \emph{for} the hotel. A multi-platform ingestion layer feeds a natural-language analysis pipeline
that scores every review for sentiment and a 0--100 reputation-risk value. A three-pass
large-language-model pipeline (Google Gemini~2.5~Flash) then distils negative and mixed reviews into a
small set of concrete, evidence-grounded, department-owned operational issues while preserving the
guest's exact specifics such as room number, amount and time. Across an analysed corpus of 2{,}939
reviews (weighted toward the reputation-relevant negative and mixed reviews), 2{,}294 problem reviews
collapsed into 117 active issues, roughly a 20:1 reduction, alongside 587 single-mention early-warning
signals, at a marginal detection cost of about US\$0.25 per full run. The report situates the system
commercially through a market analysis, a five-part feasibility study, a strategic risk analysis,
and a pricing model sized for a Sri Lankan property. We conclude that even on conservative assumptions
the platform protects a multiple of its cost, and we outline the path to production through incremental
detection and property-management-system integration.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}
Travellers no longer scroll through thousands of reviews before choosing a hotel. They ask a search
engine or an AI assistant a direct question, such as \textit{``Is The Kingsbury Colombo a good
hotel?''}, and receive a synthesised answer drawn from exactly those reviews. That answer typically
carries a caveat along the lines of \textit{``well-regarded, though some guests mention dated rooms,
noise from live music, and occasional service inconsistency.''} The caveat is not an opinion. It is a
problem that surfaced in reviews, was repeated by enough guests, and became baked into how the property
is described on every subsequent query. Online reputation has therefore become a direct commercial
asset. Cornell's analysis of lodging performance found that a one-percent improvement in a hotel's
online reputation score is associated with a 1.42\% increase in revenue per available room (RevPAR)
\citep{anderson2012social}, and more than 80\% of travellers read reviews before booking
\citep{tripadvisor2019reviews,reputationdefender2023stats}.

\subsection{Motivation}
The mechanism that now judges the hotel, an AI reading its reviews, is precisely the mechanism a hotel
should run \emph{for itself}, but sharper and earlier. A generic assistant only reports that an issue
has \emph{already} bubbled up, and by then it is a public caveat that no tool can delete. The
opportunity is to detect each operational fault at its \emph{first} mention rather than its
two-hundredth, route it to the responsible department, and fix the cause before it spreads. This is not
review deletion or damage repair. It is a standing investment in the reputation the hotel has not yet
lost.

\subsection{Problem statement}
A high-volume property generates more reviews, across more platforms and languages, than any operations
team can read, de-duplicate, risk-rank and assign consistently. The cost is not the reading time. It is
the \emph{pattern that gets missed}, the single complaint today that is silently the two-hundredth of a
recurring fault. The core problem this project addresses is the \textbf{cognitive-load and synthesis
gap}, namely converting a continuous, multi-platform stream of unstructured reviews into a short,
prioritised, evidence-backed and department-owned list of actionable issues.

\subsection{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*.}]
  \item To \textbf{ingest and normalise} guest reviews from multiple platforms (Google, TripAdvisor,
        Booking.com) into a single canonical store through modular, official-shaped connectors.
  \item To \textbf{analyse} each review for sentiment, owning department, and a transparent 0--100
        reputation-risk score.
  \item To \textbf{detect operational issues} by distilling negative and mixed reviews into a small set
        of concrete, de-duplicated, evidence-grounded issues that preserve the guest's specifics.
  \item To \textbf{prioritise and assign} each issue to a department with risk-based ranking, recurrence
        tracking, and human-in-the-loop resolution.
  \item To \textbf{evaluate the commercial case} through market, feasibility and risk analysis against a
        real five-star property.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}
The deliverable is a functioning proof-of-concept (POC) running locally, comprising a web application,
an API, and a relational database. It serves four operator roles, namely an administrator, an
operations manager, a department head, and an analyst, each with a tailored view of the same data. It
uses official-shaped connectors with sample and crawled data rather than live production credentials,
and is sized for a single property. Production concerns such as live platform authorisation,
incremental detection at full scale, and property-management integration are designed for but
deliberately out of POC scope, and are discussed in Sections~\ref{sec:risk} and~\ref{sec:lessons}.

\section{Market Analysis}
\label{sec:market}

\subsection{Current approaches and competitors}
Hotels manage reviews today in three broad ways, and each leaves a gap. \textbf{Manual triage}, where
staff read reviews platform by platform, does not scale, drifts in consistency, and never reliably
connects one complaint to the two hundred like it across a year. \textbf{Generic reputation and
social-listening tools}, including established suites such as Medallia/ReviewPro, TrustYou and Revinate,
aggregate reviews and present sentiment dashboards and word clouds, but they report broad themes such as
``people mention food'' rather than specific, owner-assignable incidents such as ``breakfast buffet ran
empty by 9\,am'', and they attach no department ownership or tracking. \textbf{Native platform AI and
general assistants} now summarise reviews directly, which is convenient for a traveller but reactive for
a hotel, because by the time a theme is prominent enough to be summarised it is already a public caveat.
Academic work on aspect-based sentiment analysis \citep{pontiki2016semeval} similarly yields aspect
categories rather than the concrete work orders an operations team can close.

\subsection{Demand and review context}
\label{sec:demand}
A reasonable question is whether a property of this size even generates enough review pressure to
warrant a dedicated tool. Table~\ref{tab:demand} sizes the hotel from its real room count of 229 and
clearly-flagged illustrative operating assumptions. At a representative 72\% occupancy and a US\$130
average daily rate, the property turns roughly 60{,}000 occupied room-nights and about US\$7.8\,M of room
revenue per year, and at an estimated average stay of about 2.5 nights it sees on the order of 65
arrivals per day. Against its roughly 20{,}000 cumulative public reviews, current review velocity is on
the order of 10 to 20 new reviews per day across platforms. In Asia, online travel agencies (OTAs), the
commission booking channels such as Booking.com, account for
roughly 70\% of \emph{online} hotel bookings \citep{asia2024ota}, and more than 80\% of travellers read
reviews before booking on \emph{any} channel \citep{tripadvisor2019reviews}, so review pressure shapes a
large share of demand for a property like this. These are not large numbers to \emph{read}, which is the
heart of a natural objection that a staff member could simply read them. But reading is not the task.
The task is \emph{synthesis}, namely connecting one complaint today to the two hundred spread across
twelve months, three platforms and several languages, scoring each consistently, and spotting the one
emerging issue among hundreds.

\subsection{Market gap and positioning}
The gap is the \emph{action layer}. No mainstream approach combines hotel-specific issue detection,
evidence and specifics down to the room number, department ownership and tracking, and early-warning
detection of single-mention signals, at a cost a single property can absorb. That combination is the
position this system takes.

\section{Proposed Solution}
\label{sec:solution}

\subsection{Overview}
The system is a three-tier application (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). A \textbf{Next.js / React} web client
presents the operator screens. A \textbf{FastAPI} service exposes a REST API and houses the ingestion
connectors, the natural-language analysis, and the issue-detection pipeline. State is persisted in
\textbf{PostgreSQL}. Heavy natural-language models run locally, covering sentiment, zero-shot department
classification, and sentence embeddings. Only the issue-detection reasoning calls an external large
language model (Google Gemini~2.5~Flash), with a deterministic offline \emph{stub} provider available
for development and testing.

\subsection{Key features}
The product surfaces four capabilities. \textbf{One inbox for everywhere} unifies Google, TripAdvisor
and Booking.com reviews in a single view. \textbf{AI issue detection} distils complaints into concrete,
named operational issues. \textbf{Reputation-risk scoring} rates every review and issue from 0 to 100
for urgency. \textbf{Owned actions} route each issue to a department, prioritise it, and track it to
resolution.

\subsection{Unique value proposition}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Turns thousands of scattered complaints into one prioritised, owned and evidenced action list.
  \item Preserves the guest's exact specifics, so an issue reads as ``room~1413, US\$60 unexplained
        charge'', not ``billing problems''.
  \item Catches an emerging problem at its first mention rather than its two-hundredth.
  \item Runs at near-zero marginal cost, about US\$0.25 for a full detection pass.
  \item Keeps a human in the loop, so the system proposes and staff decide.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Ingestion and the canonical data model}
Each platform has a dedicated \textbf{connector} that accepts the platform's own (``official-shaped'')
payload and maps it to a canonical review. No scraping is required, and a source can be swapped without
touching the analysis engine. Raw payloads are preserved verbatim, with a content hash for idempotent
and duplicate-safe ingestion, and then normalised. The data model is a linear enrichment pipeline. A raw
review becomes a normalised review, which gains a one-to-one analysis record, and may be linked as
evidence to one or more detected issues. The full schema is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:erd}.

\subsection{Natural-language analysis}
Every normalised review is scored by three local models. The first is a multilingual BERT sentiment
classifier (positive, mixed or negative). The second is a zero-shot department classifier built on
BART-MNLI \citep{lewis2020bart} over six hotel departments (Front Office, Housekeeping, Food \&
Beverage, Engineering, Guest Relations, Management). The third is a MiniLM sentence-embedding model
\citep{reimers2019sbert} that produces a 384-dimensional vector used later for de-duplication and issue
clustering.

\paragraph{Reputation-risk score.} Each review receives a transparent, auditable 0--100 reputation-risk
score, labelled low, medium, high or critical, computed as a bounded sum of interpretable factors, so a
manager can see \emph{why} a review is urgent.
\begin{equation}
\text{risk} = \min\!\Big(100,\;
\underbrace{\tfrac{5-r}{4}\cdot 30}_{\text{rating}}
+ \underbrace{(-s)\cdot 25}_{\text{sentiment}}
+ \underbrace{w_d}_{\text{dept.}}
+ \underbrace{u}_{\text{urgency}}
+ \underbrace{v}_{\text{visibility}}
+ \underbrace{\rho}_{\text{recency}}
+ \underbrace{\delta}_{\text{duplicate}}\Big)
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
Here $r$ is the star rating, $s\in[-1,1]$ the sentiment score, and $w_d$ a per-department weight
(Management highest). The term $u$ adds points for urgency words such as ``refund'', ``unsafe'' or
``manager'', $v$ for public visibility through helpful votes, a public URL or unanswered status, $\rho$
for recency, and $\delta$ for a detected duplicate. Every contributing factor is stored alongside the
score for audit.

\subsection{Issue detection through a three-pass LLM pipeline}
The core contribution is the issue-detection pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}), which runs only over
\textbf{negative and mixed} reviews, the reputation-relevant ones, in three passes.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em,style=nextline]
  \item[Pass A, Extract.] Reviews are sent to the LLM in small batches, and for each it returns a short
  problem summary, the owning department, and the concrete \emph{specifics} such as room or floor, item,
  amount and time.
  \item[Pass B, Consolidate.] All extracted problem labels are clustered into a \emph{dynamic} taxonomy
  of canonical issue types rather than a hard-coded list. Genuinely distinct severe incidents such as a
  pest sighting, theft or a safety hazard are deliberately kept separate rather than folded into a
  generic category.
  \item[Pass C, Assemble.] Each canonical type becomes one issue with an evidence-grounded description.
  A type supported by $\geq 2$ reviews becomes an \textbf{active} issue, while a single-mention type
  becomes an \textbf{emerging} early-warning signal. A stable \texttt{cluster\_key} lets the pipeline be
  re-run idempotently while preserving manual state such as assignee and resolved status.
\end{description}
The defining property is \textbf{specificity preservation}. The output is not ``billing problems'' but
``room~1413, US\$60 unexplained charge'', a closeable work order rather than a theme.

\subsection{Data and evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:data}
The analysed corpus comprises \textbf{2{,}939 reviews}. These combine \emph{real reviews crawled from
Google and TripAdvisor} for The Kingsbury with a set of \emph{engineered incident fixtures}. The corpus
was deliberately weighted toward \textbf{negative and mixed} reviews because those are the only
reputation-actionable inputs. The detection pipeline reads negative and mixed reviews exclusively, and
positive reviews yield no issues to act on. The engineered fixtures were generated from a library of
specific incident scenarios, each carrying concrete facts such as room, floor, item, amount and time, in
order to \textbf{stress-test specificity preservation}, because real crawled reviews frequently omit
such details. On authenticity, two of the three sources are \emph{verified-stay channels}, since on
Booking.com and predominantly on TripAdvisor reviews come from guests with a booking, which constrains
fabrication, whereas Google is the open channel and is treated as lower-confidence. The system also
never acts on a single unverified post, because an issue is meaningful through the \emph{aggregation} of
many independent reviews, and a human always resolves or dismisses it. The legal and data-protection
dimension of this is examined in Section~\ref{sec:legal}.

\subsection{Important implementation decisions}
Three decisions shaped the build. First, \textbf{the heavy NLP runs locally} on commodity hardware,
which keeps per-review analysis free and private, while only the issue-detection reasoning uses an
external LLM. Second, \textbf{Gemini 2.5 Flash with an offline stub fallback} gives high-quality
detection at about US\$0.25 per run, with a deterministic provider for tests and for running without a
key. Third, \textbf{a stable \texttt{cluster\_key}} makes the full rebuild idempotent, so the pipeline
can be re-run while preserving manual state such as assignee and resolved status.

\paragraph{Revenue impact and return.} A naive estimate applies the Cornell elasticity, where +1\%
reputation gives +1.42\% RevPAR \citep{anderson2012social}, to the entire US\$7.8\,M room revenue, giving
about US\$111k per 1\% of reputation protected. That overstates the effect, because not all revenue
responds to online reputation, so we estimate the impact against only the revenue base that online
reputation plausibly influences and present a range (Table~\ref{tab:revenue}). The lower bound attributes
impact only to OTA-booked room-nights, taken as about 25\% of total room-nights for a property with
substantial direct and corporate business. The upper bound recognises that more than 80\% of travellers read
reviews before booking on any channel \citep{tripadvisor2019reviews,reputationdefender2023stats}, so
reputation shapes demand well beyond the OTA slice. Each 1\% of reputation protected is therefore worth
roughly US\$28k to US\$89k per year, that is about LKR\,8.4\,M to LKR\,26.7\,M, against a year-one cost
near LKR\,440{,}000. Even on the lower bound, protecting half a percent of reputation repays the year-one cost
many times over, and the elasticity cuts both ways, so this is also the revenue at risk from a 1\%
erosion.

\subsection{Technical feasibility}
The system is built and running on a conventional stack of Next.js, FastAPI and PostgreSQL, with the
heavy models (BERT sentiment, BART zero-shot classification, MiniLM embeddings) running locally and only
the detection reasoning calling Gemini. The deterministic stub provider means the entire pipeline runs
offline without an API key, which de-risks both development and demonstration. Nothing in the design
requires specialised hardware beyond a commodity machine.

\subsection{Resource and time feasibility}
The proof-of-concept was delivered by a five-member student team within a single academic cycle using
free-tier and open-source components, so no licence costs were incurred. The same modular structure that
made the POC tractable, namely independent connectors, local models and a single detection service,
keeps the path to a production pilot short.

\subsection{Social and legal feasibility}
\label{sec:legal}
Socially, the platform improves guest experience by helping a hotel fix recurring faults faster, and it
fits existing operations as a ranked, owned action list rather than another raw feed. Legally, the design
is built to respect the constraints that a review-driven product faces. It uses official-shaped,
modular connectors rather than scraping, which keeps ingestion within platform terms of service.
Personal data is minimised and redacted in the interface, and the system stores operational issues
rather than guest profiles, which aligns it with Sri Lanka's Personal Data Protection Act and with the
GDPR principles that apply to international guests. Because the system proposes and a human decides, and
because every issue is evidence-linked, the legal exposure of acting on a fabricated review is low. The
clearest dependency is on third-party platform terms, which is treated as a first-class risk in
Section~\ref{sec:risk}.

\section{User Journeys}
\label{sec:journeys}
The product is designed around a single recurring journey for the operations team. A guest leaves a
review on any platform. The connector ingests it, the analysis scores it, and if it describes a
recurring problem the detection pipeline attaches it to an issue, or raises a new emerging signal if it
is the first of its kind. The operations manager opens the Issues page, already ranked by reputation
risk, inspects the evidence in the detail side-sheet, and assigns the issue to the responsible
department head. The department head fixes the underlying cause and marks the issue resolved, which is
recorded in the issue's event history. The same flow is shown as an interaction sequence in
Figure~\ref{fig:seqresolve}. A second journey serves the analyst, who uses the Dashboard and Reviews
filters to monitor sentiment and risk trends and to confirm that resolved issues stop recurring.

\section{Challenges Faced}
\label{sec:challenges}
The hardest problem was detection quality. An earlier embedding-and-clustering approach produced
hundreds of vague, overlapping clusters that no manager could act on. Rebuilding it as the three-pass
LLM pipeline, with a dynamic taxonomy and deliberate separation of severe distinct incidents, was what
turned the output into a small set of clean, specific issues. Preserving specificity was itself a
challenge, since a model summarising at scale tends to generalise away the room number and the amount,
which is why those facts are extracted explicitly and carried through every pass. Controlling cost while
keeping quality drove the choice of a fast, inexpensive model with an offline fallback. Finally, real
crawled reviews often lacked the concrete details needed to test specificity, which is why the corpus was
augmented with engineered incident fixtures.

\section{Community and Industry Impact}
\label{sec:impact}
Reputation is a shared asset for Sri Lanka's hospitality sector, where tourism is a major source of
foreign earnings. A tool that helps a hotel detect and fix recurring faults early protects not only that
property's revenue but also the guest experience that shapes the country's wider reputation as a
destination. Because the cost structure is low, the same capability is within reach of smaller and
independent hotels that cannot afford enterprise reputation suites, which helps level the field between
large chains and local operators.

% =========================================================================
\section{Lessons Learned and Future Work}
\label{sec:lessons}
Two lessons stood out. First, for review intelligence the value is in the action layer, not the summary,
so designing around owned, evidenced, early action from the start was the decision that mattered most.
Second, evaluating on data with concrete specifics early would have surfaced the specificity-preservation
requirement sooner. The principal next steps are a full detection run over the hotel's complete real
review history, the incremental-detection path that bounds LLM cost at production scale, property-management
integration to corroborate complaints against actual folios and convert flagged issues into verified
ones, and multi-property support to amortise the platform across a hotel group.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This report presented a working proof-of-concept that turns a high-volume, multi-platform stream of guest
reviews into a short, prioritised, evidence-grounded and department-owned list of operational issues. On
an analysed corpus of 2{,}939 reviews the system compressed 2{,}294 problem reviews into 117 active
issues, a roughly 20:1 reduction, plus 587 early-warning signals, at a marginal cost of about US\$0.25
per run, while preserving the guest's exact specifics down to room numbers and billed amounts. The market,
feasibility and risk analyses show a defensible niche with a low, realistic price for a Sri Lankan
property and a revenue-protection value that dwarfs that price. A hotel cannot delete what an algorithm
has already learned about its past, but for cents per analysis it can ensure tomorrow's problems never
become the next permanent caveat.
